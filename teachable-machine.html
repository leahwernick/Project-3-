Teachable Machine

Google's Teachable Machine is a free, browser-based tool that lets anyone train a basic machine learning model — no coding experience required. You provide examples, the model learns from them, and it can then classify new inputs in real time. It's an accessible entry point into understanding how AI and machine learning actually work.

This kind of hands-on tool helps reveal something important: machine learning models are only as good as the data they're trained on. Who provides the examples? Whose faces, voices, or gestures are included? These questions connect directly to the themes of bias, power, and representation we explore in LIS 500.

Project Statement

Creating our own machine learning classification model using Google's Teachable Machine has been both a technical and reflective experience. Beyond building an algorithm capable of recognizing images, this project forced us to think critically about the data that powers these systems and therefore think about the broader implications of artificial intelligence in society. By collecting original data, training a model, and evaluating its performance, we gained firsthand insight into how machine learning systems are constructed and where their limitations lie. At the same time, reflecting on Unmasking AI by Joy Buolamwini helped contextualize our experience within ongoing discussions about bias, fairness, and accountability in AI.

The most important part of this project was collecting data. Each group member uploaded orginal samples which were photos without our hands in the camera frame, with our hands, and holding up a peace sign. The goal was to get the machine to recognize our hand movement. While collecting the data we thought carefully about what "original" data means and how consistency affects model performance. For example, we had not considered that when collecting our images the lighting conditions, background environment, and most importantly variations in subject, greatly affects the way the model works. Quickly we learned that the more consistent the images were with the context of the subject using the model the better the machine worked.

When we tested the model, we observed that it could correctly classify many of our inputs, but it also made mistakes, especially when presented with unfamiliar conditions. These errors highlighted the limitations of machine learning systems and the importance of understanding how they are trained.

The biggest takeaway from this project was how sensitive the machine learning models are. The smallest imbalances in the dataset lead to noticable differences in performance. For example, if one category had more data and more varied examples the model became better at recognizing that category and struggled more with the others. This experience directly connects to the themes discussed in Unmasking AI, particularly the idea that biased or incomplete datasets can lead to biased outcomes. Although our project was relatively small in scale, it demonstrated how easily these issues can arise.

In Unmasking AI, Joy Buolamwini discusses how facial recognition systems have historically performed poorly on individuals with darker skin tones due to a lack of diverse training data. While our project did not involve facial recognition specifically, we encountered a similar principle: the model can only learn from what it sees. If certain variations are not represented in the dataset, the algorithm will not perform well when encountering them. This realization made us more aware of the responsibility that comes with designing and training AI systems. Even though a model may seem functioning to it's creator it may not be inclusive and represenative of the general public.

Another key takeaway from the book that resonated with our experience is the concept of the "coded gaze," which refers to how the perspectives and biases of developers can influence AI systems. In our project, we made decisions about what categories to include, how to label data, and which examples to collect. These choices shaped the behavior of our model. For example, if we unintentionally favored certain types of images or conditions, the model reflected those biases in its predictions. This reinforced the idea that AI systems are not neutral but rather they are shaped by human decisions at every stage of development.

Additionally, the project helped us understand the importance of transparency and documentation in machine learning. By uploading our dataset to GitHub, we made our work accessible for others to review and replicate. This aligns with the broader call in Unmasking AI for greater accountability in AI development. When systems are not transparent it becomes difficult to identify and address biases or errors.

Additionally, we reflected on how machine learning systems operate in real-world scenarios. Our model was created for an academic assignment but similar technologies are used in areas like hiring, healthcare, and law enforcement. The errors or biases in those systems can have serious consequences and through our project we gained a deeper appreciation for the need to thoroughly test AI systems before they are used in these high stake situations.

The process of creating a video demonstration of our model further reinstated these ideas. Showcasing the model in action we were able to truly see the strengths and weaknesses of our machine. In many cases the model perfomed well but when variables changed the model struggled.

In conclusion, this project provided valuable insights into both the technical and ethical dimensions of machine learning. By building our own classification model, we gained hands-on experience with data collection, training, and evaluation. At the same time, reflecting on Unmasking AI helped us understand the broader implications of our work. We learned that data quality and diversity are critical, that human biases can influence AI systems, and that transparency and accountability are essential for responsible AI development. These lessons will continue to shape how we approach technology in the future, reminding us to be both thoughtful creators and critical users of AI systems.
