Teachable Machine

Google's Teachable Machine is a free, browser-based tool that lets anyone train a basic machine learning model — no coding experience required. You provide examples, the model learns from them, and it can then classify new inputs in real time. It's an accessible entry point into understanding how AI and machine learning actually work.

This kind of hands-on tool helps reveal something important: machine learning models are only as good as the data they're trained on. Who provides the examples? Whose faces, voices, or gestures are included? These questions connect directly to the themes of bias, power, and representation we explore in LIS 500.

Project Statement

The process of creating our own machine learning classification model using Google's Teachable Machine has been both a technical and reflective experience. This project required us to not only build an algorithm capable of recognizing images or sounds, but also to critically think about the data that powers such systems and the broader implications of artificial intelligence in society. By collecting original data, training a model, and evaluating its performance, we gained firsthand insight into how machine learning systems are constructed and where their limitations lie. At the same time, reflecting on Unmasking AI by Joy Buolamwini helped contextualize our experience within ongoing discussions about bias, fairness, and accountability in AI.

Our project began with the data collection phase, which was one of the most important and challenging components. Each group member contributed 100 original samples, resulting in a dataset of several hundred images. This requirement forced us to think carefully about what "original" data means and how consistency affects model performance. For example, when collecting images, we had to consider lighting conditions, camera angles, background environments, and variations in the subject. Initially, we assumed that more data would automatically lead to better results, but we quickly learned that the quality and diversity of the data were just as important as quantity. If all images were taken in the same setting, the model would perform well in that specific context but fail when exposed to new environments.

Once we compiled our dataset, we used Teachable Machine to train our classification model. The platform made it relatively easy to upload images, label them, and generate a working model. However, the simplicity of the interface masked the complexity of what was happening behind the scenes. The algorithm analyzed patterns in the data and attempted to distinguish between categories based on features it learned during training. When we tested the model, we observed that it could correctly classify many of our inputs, but it also made mistakes, especially when presented with unfamiliar conditions. These errors highlighted the limitations of machine learning systems and the importance of understanding how they are trained.

One of the most significant lessons we learned from this process was how sensitive machine learning models are to the data they are given. Even small imbalances in the dataset could lead to noticeable differences in performance. For instance, if one category had more varied examples than another, the model would become better at recognizing that category while struggling with others. This experience directly connects to the themes discussed in Unmasking AI, particularly the idea that biased or incomplete datasets can lead to biased outcomes. Although our project was relatively small in scale, it demonstrated how easily these issues can arise.

In Unmasking AI, Joy Buolamwini discusses how facial recognition systems have historically performed poorly on individuals with darker skin tones due to a lack of diverse training data. While our project did not involve facial recognition specifically, we encountered a similar principle: the model can only learn from what it sees. If certain variations are not represented in the dataset, the algorithm will not perform well when encountering them. This realization made us more aware of the responsibility that comes with designing and training AI systems. It is not enough to simply build a functioning model; we must also ensure that the data used to train it is representative and inclusive.

Another key takeaway from the book that resonated with our experience is the concept of the "coded gaze," which refers to how the perspectives and biases of developers can influence AI systems. In our project, we made decisions about what categories to include, how to label data, and which examples to collect. These choices shaped the behavior of our model. For example, if we unintentionally favored certain types of images or conditions, the model reflected those biases in its predictions. This reinforced the idea that AI systems are not neutral — they are shaped by human decisions at every stage of development.

Additionally, the project helped us understand the importance of transparency and documentation in machine learning. By uploading our dataset and code to GitHub, we made our work accessible for others to review and replicate. This aligns with the broader call in Unmasking AI for greater accountability in AI development. When systems are opaque, it becomes difficult to identify and address biases or errors. By contrast, open and well-documented projects allow for collaboration and improvement.

We also reflected on the ethical implications of deploying machine learning systems in real-world contexts. While our model was created for an academic assignment, similar technologies are used in areas such as hiring, law enforcement, and healthcare. Errors or biases in these systems can have serious consequences. Through our project, we gained a deeper appreciation for the need to thoroughly test and evaluate AI systems before they are used in high-stakes situations. This includes not only measuring accuracy but also examining how the model performs across different groups and conditions.

Another lesson from Unmasking AI that stood out to us is the importance of questioning the assumptions behind AI technologies. It is easy to view machine learning as an objective and reliable tool, but our experience showed that it is far from perfect. The model sometimes produced confident but incorrect predictions, which could be misleading if users are not aware of its limitations. This highlights the need for critical thinking when interacting with AI systems. Users should not blindly trust the outputs but instead consider how the system was trained and what its limitations might be.

The process of embedding a video demonstration of our model further reinforced these ideas. By showcasing the model in action, we were able to observe its strengths and weaknesses more clearly. In some cases, the model performed well under controlled conditions but struggled when variables changed. This visual demonstration made it easier to communicate the challenges of building reliable machine learning systems and underscored the importance of continuous testing and refinement.

In conclusion, this project provided valuable insights into both the technical and ethical dimensions of machine learning. By building our own classification model, we gained hands-on experience with data collection, training, and evaluation. At the same time, reflecting on Unmasking AI helped us understand the broader implications of our work. We learned that data quality and diversity are critical, that human biases can influence AI systems, and that transparency and accountability are essential for responsible AI development. These lessons will continue to shape how we approach technology in the future, reminding us to be both thoughtful creators and critical users of AI systems.
